UC Berkeley Professor Argues AI Development Is Moving Too Fast, Even at the Cost of Cancer Cures

An assistant professor of computer science at UC Berkeley wrote an essay in The Atlantic on June 21, 2026, titled "I'd Rather Risk Cancer Than See AI Move This Fast," making the case that the pace of AI development is too dangerous to justify even transformative medical benefits. The piece references Dario Amodei's 2024 essay "Machines of Loving Grace," which predicted superhuman AI could compress a century of scientific progress into a decade and cut cancer mortality by 95%. The author, who identifies a personal cancer risk, nonetheless argues that the speed of AI advancement poses risks that outweigh those potential gains. (Source)

Why it matters: The essay reflects a growing fracture within the AI and computer science community over whether transformative benefits justify rapid, potentially unsafe development. Its publication in The Atlantic brings the AI safety debate to a broad general audience at a moment when AI capabilities are accelerating sharply.

Trump Blames Vandalism for Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool Problems, Offers No Evidence

President Trump attributed deterioration at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool to vandalism, though AP reporting found no substantiation for the claim. Images from June 19, 2026 show a missing section of blue coating along the pool's edge and crews using vacuum equipment to pump water from the pool. The pool's condition has drawn public attention, and Trump's unsubstantiated vandalism claim adds a political dimension to what may be a maintenance or algae-related issue. The AP published the story on June 20, 2026. (Source)

Why it matters: An unsubstantiated presidential claim about vandalism at a national monument raises questions about accountability for the upkeep of federally managed landmarks and sets a precedent for how infrastructure failures at iconic sites may be publicly characterized.

U.S. Graduation Season Closes Amid Wave of Controversies Over Speeches, AI, and Booing

As the 2026 U.S. graduation season draws to a close, USA Today reports a wave of controversies at high school and college ceremonies across the country. Issues flagged include censored or restricted student speeches, incidents involving artificial intelligence, and disruptive audience behavior such as booing. At least one event in Washington state, reported by the Tri-City Herald, drew local coverage. The pattern of controversy at graduation ceremonies reflects ongoing tensions around free expression, technology use, and decorum at milestone public events. (Source)

Why it matters: Recurring controversies at U.S. graduation ceremonies reflect deeper societal tensions around free speech, AI in education, and community norms — issues that will only intensify as AI tools become more embedded in academic life.

Today in History: June 21 — U.S. Constitution Ratified 238 Years Ago

June 21 marks the anniversary of the U.S. Constitution entering into force in 1788, when New Hampshire became the required ninth state to ratify it. The date also coincides with the summer solstice in 2026. Additional historical events noted on this date include Cyrus Hall McCormick receiving a patent for his mechanical reaper in 1834, and the opening of the first Ferris wheel at the Chicago World's Fair in 1893. The Associated Press compiled these milestones as part of its regular "Today in History" feature. (Source)

Why it matters: The U.S. Constitution's ratification on this date in 1788 established the foundational legal document governing the world's oldest surviving federal democracy, a milestone worth annual reflection. These historical touchstones provide context for understanding how American institutions and technology developed.
